Hey guys, today I want to talk about a solution I found for fixing elbow dip, and maybe any problem when it comes to form work.

I'll give a quick intro as to who I am for context, and then I'll get into the solution and why I think it works.

My name is Ryan, and I have played disc golf for about 5 years now. A couple of years ago I started getting interested in backhand
form, since forehand only got a little boring. I don't need more than 300ft of distance for the courses I play the majority of the time, but it would be fun to have it.

As for my sports background I grew up playing ball golf. I played all the standard little league sports (baseball, ice hockey, roller hockey, soccer, etc) and a little tennis in highschool, but for the most part I was a golfer. I don't want to bore you with details on my golf, so I'll
just say I played for about 12 years and carded a few tournament rounds in the 60's.

So, I thought, "hey, I've learned how to hit a golf ball pretty well, learning a backhand should be pretty easy for me". I was wrong.

Not only did I underestimate the value of learning a sport as a kid, I also underestimated how athletic of a movement the disc golf backhand is, but more on that later.

Now, for whatever reason, my body had a really bad case of elbow dip. I think it's related to the fact that a golf swing is a very downard motion
where the disc golf swing is not (in fact it's a slightly upward motion).

Regardless, I got some lessons from Overthrow Disc Golf and they were really good. But I ran into a problem.

They would tell me what was wrong, and they would give me drills to fix them, but they didn't give me an exact layout for what practice sessions should look like, and how many I should aim to do before the next coaching session.

Now look, every student is different and requires different advice. I'm not saying that because they didn't give me an excact regimen that they are bad coaches.

In fact, I'd go so far as to say they were great coaches but I was a bad student. I knew roughly what I needed to do, but without a super defined plan for how to get there, I lost motivation.

It could absolutely be part of their process to leave the "how many reps" and "how often" and any other questions about practice open ended so that their students find what works best for them. But I wish I would have asked for a starter template. Something to try and that I could tweak where I saw fit.

That's what I have for you today. This is what I did to fix my elbow dip and it has worked fantastically. It still creeps back in a little bit on big throws, but for the most part it is completely gone.

## Training:

### 5-6 days a week, 2-3 times per day

- 1st Set: 10 throws on one foot
- 2nd Set: 10 throws at a stand still
- 3rd Set: 10 throws with a one step
- 4th Set: 3-5 throws with a walkup

- I would typically check form with a slomo on 1 or 2 of each set. Enough to keep track of what my form looks like, but not so much that I'm completely reliant on the video. "Feel" is a big part of the learning process. Video is just a reality check.

One thing this really helped me with is burnout. This regimen limits you to 35 throws max per session, 3 sessions max per day.

If you're anything like me, I would have 1-2 hour long session where I would get really tired, really frustrated, and eventually burnout after 1 or 2 weeks. This stops that.

Also, let's say I did 6 days and did this twice a day. That's about 420 reps in one week. But here's the interesting part.

It's not really about the 420 number. It's more about the number of days of consistent practice.

There's a few things that happen when you are consistent with something like this like

1. Subconcious Processing: when you're working on something just a little bit every day, your brain does a lot in the background to help cement what you are trying to learn. This is something you can't get by just doing a bunch of reps in a short amount of time.

2. Noticing Contributing Factors (for me, I noticed my left arm was flying high in the air, and that was encouraging elbow dip. I also noticed I was reaching back super downward and starting with my arm way too low, which also encouraged elbow dip)

3. Slow but steady speed progression: at first you might only be able to perform a form change at 40% power. Then, as the days go by, you are slowly able to do it faster and faster, until it's just "how you throw".

Anyway, I just think it's super useful to have some kind of plan in place execute.
